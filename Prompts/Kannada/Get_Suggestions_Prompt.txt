A NOTE ON HOOKED WORDS AND LINKING WORDS
The reference below speaks of a "hooked word" and a "linking word". The hooked word is the word the thought is anchored to just before a pause - usually a verb or a noun that completes the main thought early. The linking word is the word right after the pause that carries the thought across it and reconnects the next chunk to the hooked word - a "because", a "that is", a pronoun, a "then", a "not".

Never write any tags or labels around these words in your output. Your suggestions must be clean Kannada text with no tags of any kind.

Example 1: The cause-and-effect inversion
English: The boy felt so ashamed, so pained because he let down his guru.
Analysis: Standard Kannada grammar places the cause before the effect ("because he let down his guru, he was so pained"). Translated that way, the pause structure and the suspense of why he is pained are lost. Instead, complete the primary thought at a hooked word ("…he was so pained"), then bridge the pause with a linking word ("because…"), and give the reason after it. The listener receives the emotional impact first and the reason second, mirroring the English delivery.

Example 2: The suspenseful relative clause
English: If you were a good manager of this factory you would create chemistry which would make you blissful and ecstatic.
Analysis: In traditional written Kannada, the relative clause is front-loaded ("you would create a chemistry that keeps you blissful"). This destroys the dramatic pause after "chemistry". Instead, turn the verb into an open-ended hook ("you would create such a chemistry…"), and follow the pause with a pronoun link ("…that it keeps you blissful and ecstatic"). The complex thought becomes two digestible chunks, and the pause stays exactly where the speaker put it.

Example 3: The grand reveal
English: Well, the word drink, drunk people understand it comes from a bottle. But it is a fact. It's a very well established scientific fact today that the greatest and the most sophisticated chemical factory on the planet is right here. This is it.
Analysis: English uses placeholder structures ("It is a fact… that…"). Kannada does not naturally use placeholders; it prefers to state the whole fact and conclude with "…is a fact" at the very end. To prevent the listener from waiting until the end of a very long sentence to know what the statement is, hook onto the word "fact" and bridge with a linking phrase meaning "and that is this:" before the content. The listener digests "this is a fact" before stepping across the pause into the meat of the information.

English: We are not talking about different dimensions of life which actually make this life work at its best.
Analysis: In standard written Kannada the descriptive clause ("which actually make this life work at its best") would be placed before the noun it describes. Doing so destroys the English pause placement. Instead, end the main clause early with its verb ("…we are talking about different dimensions of life"), keep the pause, and reattach the trailing thought with a linking word that reminds the listener it is still about those dimensions ("…about the ones that actually make this life work at its best").
