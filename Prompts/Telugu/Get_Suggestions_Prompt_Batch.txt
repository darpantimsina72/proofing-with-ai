READING THE REVIEWER'S NOTE
The note says what is wrong, and every one of the three versions must fix exactly that. Notes come in a few common kinds:
  - the flow is broken, or the sentence reads like separate chunks;
  - an ending is not connected to the rest of the sentence;
  - "Setence seem very literal…" (Real Meaning): the sentence carries the words but not the intention. Fix the intention and keep the sentence's structure. Do not replace Sadhguru's own image or plain words with your interpretation of them, and do not split the sentence unless the meaning needs it;
  - "the word used seems not contextually rich…" (Right word): the word does not say exactly what is meant here. The fix may be a more specific word, the word in the right sense, this book's term, the Telugu idiom, Sadhguru's own image put back, or an English word Telugu speakers use - and sometimes a plainer or more literal word. These marks are usually one or two words: return one or two words;
  - the sentence is too long and should be made crisper without losing any detail;
  - or the reviewer's own words, describing anything else. Follow those exactly — if the note asks for something specific, all three versions do it.

If the marked word is already this book's term and the only problem is that a reader may not recognise it, Version 1 may keep the word and add the English in brackets: వ్యక్తిత్వం (personality).

SOME NOTES DO NOT NAME THE PROBLEM
A note may simply say that the sentence or phrase needs to be evaluated, without saying what is actually wrong with it. Whenever a note does not name a problem, diagnose it yourself first. Read the Telugu on its own, then against the English, and work out whether it is:
  - broken flow, or an ending that does not connect;
  - a literal translation that misses what is actually meant;
  - a generic word in place of a contextually rich one;
  - a dictionary word where Telugu uses a different one in this context, or a near-synonym that does not fit how Telugu says it here;
  - English sentence structure carried into the Telugu;
  - register: a rare, learned word in a teaching passage, a stiff formal word in a story or dialogue, or something crude or slangy;
  - or a problem of faithfulness: something in the English that is missing, something added that is not in the English, or a meaning that has shifted (a lost "not", the wrong subject, a different tense or condition).
Then fix what you found. Do not report the diagnosis — just return the three versions.


HOW TO READ THE ENGLISH
The English is there so you understand exactly what is being said and what the speaker intends. Every version must say what the English says — no less and no more. Where the note is about something missing or shifted, the English is what you restore it from.

Its punctuation and sentence structure carry no information for you. Do not copy the English full stops, commas, hyphens or word order into the Telugu. Write the Telugu the way Telugu wants to be written.


PUNCTUATION IN THE TELUGU
This is a book. Punctuation in the Telugu - full stops, commas, … ; and - - is ordinary written punctuation. It does NOT mark pauses, timing or audio of any kind. Keep the existing punctuation where it is fine, and change it only where the fix you are making needs it to change.


THE THREE VERSIONS
The three versions are three different degrees of change, from the least to the most. They are NOT ranked - the reviewer picks the one that suits the book. Every one of the three must fix the issue in the note. A version that leaves the problem in place is not a valid version.

Version 1 - the smallest change.
Copy the marked text word for word and change only the words that carry the problem in the note. Every other word, ending, connective, spelling and bracket, and the word order, stays as it is - even a word you would not have chosen yourself. Do not swap a correct word for a synonym, make a word plainer, replace a loan word, add a యొక్క, or reorder, unless that is the problem. If the note names a replacement, use exactly that replacement.
"Smallest" is not "closest to the English wording". The words Version 1 brings in must be the ones a Telugu writer uses for this sense here: the Telugu idiom for an English idiom, the verb that fits the context, this book's term. Never a word-for-word rendering of the English word or phrase, a word built from its parts (అనుభవ పరిధి for "exposure", అనాహ్లాదకరం for "unpleasant"), its word order or its sentence breaks. Use an English word, in Telugu script, only for a modern or technical thing Telugu readers know by that name (ప్రోగ్రామ్, మైక్రోస్కోప్) - never for an idea. Where the note is about meaning or faithfulness, Version 1 restores the English meaning - in Telugu words.
Version 1 must still read as correct Telugu: if your change needs a neighbouring word to change with it (agreement, a case ending - a subject changed to అవి takes గ్రహించగలవు, not గ్రహించగలరు), change that too. If the note names a problem of structure - no main verb, a subject dropped and picked up again, కలిగి ఉంది, an English phrase shape - Version 1 rebuilds that clause; swapping one word inside it is not a fix.

Version 2 - the most natural reading flow.
Rework the sentence as a fluent Telugu writer would write it, so it reads smoothly in the context of the page, while keeping exactly the meaning of the English. Re-order clauses, merge or split phrases, and choose better words as needed.

Version 3 - the freest idiomatic Telugu.
The turn of phrase a native Telugu writer would reach for - often a Telugu idiom. Restructure as needed; split a long sentence into shorter ones only when that is what makes it clear. It may move furthest from the English structure - but never from its meaning, and it must not add anything that is not in the English.

For a mark of one or two words, all three versions are one or two words, and each is a different choice: Version 1 the word a Telugu writer uses for this sense, Version 2 a natural alternative, Version 3 the Telugu idiom if there is one.


LANGUAGE
Write clear, natural, modern written Telugu - the language of a good Telugu book today.
- Use THIS BOOK'S TERMS exactly.
- For every word you bring in, decide which sense the English word has in THIS sentence and write the Telugu word for that sense. Abstract nouns, adjectives and small words go wrong most: "distinction" - తేడా, not విభజన; "I see that…" meaning I know - తెలుసు; "the terrible rut" - ఊబి, not చక్రం.
- No English phrase shape: a set phrase has its own Telugu phrase ("far from the truth" - ఏమాత్రం నిజం కాదు; "the moment X" - X-న మరుక్షణం; "predict doom" - ఏదో ఘోరం జరగబోతోందని జోస్యం చెప్పడం).
- Keep the mood of the English: "it should be me" (bound to be) - అది నేనే అయి ఉండాలి, not కావాలి (a wish); "can be" - కాగలదు, not "is"; "This is one reason" - ఇది ఒక కారణం, not అందుకే.
- Teaching passages: clear written Telugu, without rare learned words a general reader would stumble on. Sadhguru's concept terms are not such words.
- Stories, jokes, dialogue and asides: everyday Telugu, with the English words Telugu speakers actually use there (జోక్, ప్రోగ్రామ్, బిజీ, మిస్ అవ్వడం). Never crude or slangy.
- Sadhguru's "you" is మీరు in every version - మీ, మిమ్మల్ని, మీకు, and verbs like చేస్తారు, చేయండి - even in a story or a joke. Never write నువ్వు, నీ, నిన్ను or a నువ్వు verb form (చేస్తావు, చెయ్యి) for it. Keep a నువ్వు only where the marked text already has it between a story's own characters.
- Add nothing the English does not have: no intensifier, no emphatic -ఏ, no కూడా / కలిసి / ఇక / మళ్ళీ, no adjective, no explanation, no new యొక్క, and do not name what a pronoun refers to unless the English names it or the Telugu would be unclear without it. Say a spoken restatement once.
- Keep every small qualifier of the English in all three versions - anymore (ఇక), also (కూడా), even (ఆఖరికి), even if (…అయినా సరే), altogether (పూర్తిగా), like (లాంటిది), own (సొంత), much deeper (మరింత లోతుగా). Versions 2 and 3 must not lose them while rewriting.
