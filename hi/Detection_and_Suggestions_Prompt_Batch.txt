Pass 1 - THE HINDI ON ITS OWN. Do not look at the English yet. Read the page the way a Hindi reader would, and identify where the reading stumbles:

1. Flow
Identify the sentences that read like broken chunks or are not properly closed. Often Sadhguru first states the main point and then adds to it, or says one long sentence that adds detail after detail before it closes. When a translation keeps every segment in the English order, the Hindi can read as a string of fragments. Identify these.

2. Intention
Identify the sentences or phrases that seem like the odd man in the room - they do not naturally say something related to the context around them. These are usually literal translations that do not carry what the speaker actually meant.

3. Generic words in place of contextually rich words
A word that is correct but flat, where a more specific word would convey what is actually meant.

4. An ending that is not connected to the rest of the sentence
The last part or phrase of the sentence does not connect to what came before it in the sentence.

5. Register
This is a book, so the Hindi should read as natural, modern written Hindi - clear and easy to read, the way a good Hindi book of today reads. Flag two kinds of problem:
- words or phrases that are needlessly formal, archaic or Sanskritised (Grandhika) where a plainer word would serve.
  Example: "Phenomena" translated as "परिघटना" should be flagged in favour of a plainer word such as "अद्भुत घटना".
- the opposite: phrasing that is so casual or slangy that it reads like transcribed speech rather than a book.

6. Contextual phrasing and action shifts
Phrases where a direct translation slightly shifts the meaning, emphasis or idiom of the action.
Example: "Great storyteller" translated as "महान कहानियाँ सुनाने वाले" (teller of great stories) misses the action. It should be flagged to change to "कहानियाँ बहुत बढ़िया सुनाने वाले" (telling stories greatly).
Example: "Never be the same" translated as "एक जैसा नहीं रहेगा" (will not be identical) should be flagged to change to "पहले जैसा नहीं रहेगा" (will not be like before).

7. A dictionary word where Hindi uses a different one (colloquial word, not the literal one)
The Hindi uses the word-for-word dictionary equivalent of the English word, but in this context a Hindi speaker would naturally use a different word. The word is not wrong in general - it is wrong here. Work out from the context what is actually being referred to, then ask what Hindi calls THAT. This is not the same as type 3: there the word is right but flat; here it is the wrong word for this context.
Example: "snakes and their symbols", where the symbols are carved or installed images of snakes, translated as "साँपों के निशान". निशान means marks or signs; for images of a snake Hindi says "मूर्तियाँ" or "प्रतिमाएँ".

8. Context-appropriate synonyms
Of two near-synonyms, the Hindi has used the one that does not fit how Hindi says it in this context. Watch especially the everyday verbs - of saying, telling, calling, knowing, seeing, making, doing - where English uses one word and Hindi chooses between several.
Example: "they say" in the sense of how something is called, or what people commonly hold to be true, is "कहते हैं" - not "बताते हैं". बताना is telling something to someone; कहना is how a name, a saying or a common belief is reported.


9. English sentence structure carried into the Hindi
The words are right, but they stand in the English order, so the sentence reads like a translation. Hindi has its own structure and it comes first: what qualifies something - the reason, cause, condition, time, place or manner - usually comes before what it qualifies, and the main point settles at the end with the verb.
Example: "This is very important for many reasons" translated as "यह कई कारणों से बहुत महत्वपूर्ण है" keeps the English order. Hindi says "कई कारणों से यह बहुत महत्वपूर्ण है".
But a sentence does not stand alone. Keep its joins with its neighbours intact: what links back to the previous sentence stays near the start, and what the next sentence picks up stays where the next sentence can reach it. Do not reorder a sentence into one that reads well by itself but no longer flows from the sentence before or into the sentence after - when the two pull against each other, find the order that satisfies both.
Flag only when the English order actually makes the Hindi read like a translation - not when it is merely one of several natural Hindi orders.

11. Additions

12. Meaning shifts
